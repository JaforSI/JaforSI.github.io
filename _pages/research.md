Climate extremes, rising seas and degraded landscapes do not hit all people the same way, and the differences show up in the most intimate places: whether a family has another child, whether a household moves or stays, whether a child gets sick. Yet we still measure these outcomes in separate boxes, one survey, one hazard, one discipline at a time. If the pathways are connected, what makes them diverge across places? And which landscape investments actually protect people?

To tackle this, I combine household and population surveys, remote sensing and mixed methods to explain how environmental change and environmental protection shape demographic and health outcomes. My work moves between three questions: how climate shocks change family formation, how we can measure vulnerability in a way that holds up locally, and how restoring landscapes can improve human health.

## I. Climate shocks and family formation

Cyclones and floods are often treated as one category of "climate disaster". In rural Bangladesh they are not. I compare communities hit by cyclones with communities hit by floods to ask how each hazard changes fertility intentions, family structure and perceived child mortality risk. Combining survey data with in-depth interviews, this work shows that the type of hazard matters for reproductive decisions, and that household vulnerability, land ownership and family structure sit between the shock and the decision.

A synthesis of the disaster and fertility literature, and country-level work on total fertility rate, place these household findings in a wider demographic frame.

## II. Measuring vulnerability, migration and adaptation

Indices are only useful if they reflect how people actually live with risk. I build household-level climate vulnerability measures for cyclone-prone and flood-prone areas, and compare what people perceive with what meteorological records show. The same lens is applied to migration: who moves, who stays, and what coping strategies each group relies on after an extreme weather event.

Ahmed, Tan & Rudd 2026. *Climate and Development*. Are communities in a cyclone-prone area more vulnerable than those in a flood-prone area? Towards a climate vulnerability index for rural households in Bangladesh. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/17565529.2026.2715692)

Ahmed, Haq & Hyder 2023. *Frontiers in Environmental Science*. The decision to migrate and coping strategies in the context of an extreme weather event in Bangladesh. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fenvs.2023.1160394)

## III. Landscapes, water and health

My current work, as a postdoctoral fellow at the Smithsonian Conservation Biology Institute embedded with The Nature Conservancy's Water Funds program, asks whether protecting and restoring upstream landscapes improves health downstream. Water funds pay for conservation in a watershed, usually to secure water supply. Their health co-benefits are plausible but rarely measured.

**Health co-benefits in the Upper Tana-Nairobi Water Fund.** In Kenya I am helping build the health impact pathways that link watershed restoration to human and livestock health, and the investment case for valuing those benefits.

**Rainfall extremes and child diarrhea.** In Bangladesh, with household survey and remote sensing data, I am studying how precipitation extremes relate to diarrheal disease among children under five over a 22-year period (in preparation).

**Upstream landscapes and child health across Africa.** Building on Herrera and colleagues' analysis of upstream tree cover and child health in 35 countries, I am designing an analysis of Demographic and Health Surveys across Africa, 2000 to 2022, to ask how the link between upstream tree cover, diarrhea and stunting differs between regions (in development).
